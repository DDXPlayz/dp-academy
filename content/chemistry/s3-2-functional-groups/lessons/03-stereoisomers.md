## Cis–trans isomerism

A C=C double bond cannot rotate (the π bond would break). If **each** carbon of the double bond carries two **different** groups, two arrangements are possible:

- **cis:** the same groups on the same side;
- **trans:** the same groups on opposite sides.

Cis and trans isomers have different physical properties (e.g. boiling point) and sometimes different chemical properties. Ring compounds such as 1,2-dichlorocyclopropane show the same isomerism, because a ring also prevents rotation.

## Optical isomerism

```keyidea
A carbon with four different groups attached is chiral. The molecule and its mirror image cannot be superimposed — they are enantiomers, a pair of optical isomers.
```

Example: butan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ — C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and C₂H₅.

**Properties of enantiomers:**

- identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, solubility) and chemical properties with non-chiral reagents;
- they **rotate plane-polarised light** by equal amounts in **opposite** directions (measured with a polarimeter);
- they may react differently with other chiral molecules — important for drugs and enzymes.

A **racemic mixture** (50 : 50) shows no net rotation because the effects cancel.

## Summary

- Stereoisomers: same connectivity, different 3D arrangement.
- Cis–trans: restricted rotation (C=C or ring) and two different groups on each carbon.
- Optical: chiral carbon with four different groups; enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light oppositely; racemic mixtures are optically inactive.
