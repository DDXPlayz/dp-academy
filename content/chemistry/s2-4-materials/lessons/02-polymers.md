## Polymers and monomers

A **polymer** is a very long molecule made of many repeating units (**monomers**) joined by covalent bonds. Natural polymers include proteins, starch, cellulose and DNA; synthetic ones include poly(ethene), PVC and nylon.


## Addition polymers

Monomers with a C=C double bond join as the double bonds open:

```figure
src: chemistry/_sme/from-models-to-materials/img/fig-4a37d25e7a606db7.png
caption: Addition polymerisation: the C=C double bond of the monomer opens up and the monomers join into a chain.
```

```figure
src: chemistry/_sme/from-models-to-materials/img/fig-22bbd12751de7570.png
caption: Polymer formulas use brackets around the repeating unit with n outside: ethene gives poly(ethene), chloroethene gives PVC.
```

$$
n\,\text{CH}_2\text{=CH}_2 \rightarrow -[\text{CH}_2\text{–CH}_2]_n-
$$

```keyidea
In addition polymerisation one bond of each C=C breaks and the monomers join in a chain; the polymer is the only product. The repeating unit has the same atoms as the monomer.
```

| Monomer | Polymer | Repeating unit |
|---|---|---|
| ethene CH₂=CH₂ | poly(ethene) | –CH₂–CH₂– |
| propene CH₂=CHCH₃ | poly(propene) | –CH₂–CH(CH₃)– |
| chloroethene CH₂=CHCl | poly(chloroethene), PVC | –CH₂–CHCl– |
| tetrafluoroethene CF₂=CF₂ | PTFE | –CF₂–CF₂– |

```example
Draw the repeating unit of poly(propene).
---
–[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]– : two carbons in the backbone joined by a single bond, with a CH₃ branch on one of them and bonds extending through the brackets.
```

## Plastics and the environment

Most addition polymers are **non-biodegradable** — their strong C–C and C–H bonds resist breakdown. Issues: landfill, ocean microplastics, and toxic gases (e.g. HCl from PVC) when burned. Responses: reduce use, **recycle**, use **biodegradable** polymers, and recover energy by incineration with gas scrubbing.

```figure

caption: Polymer chains are held together by weak intermolecular forces between them, while the bonds within each chain are strong covalent bonds.
```

## (HL) Condensation polymers

Monomers with **two functional groups** react with each other, releasing a small molecule (usually H₂O) at each link.

```figure
src: chemistry/_sme/from-models-to-materials/img/fig-40c55f71d50bc15a.png
caption: A polyester: monomers join through ester links, releasing water each time (condensation polymerisation).
```

```figure
src: chemistry/_sme/from-models-to-materials/img/fig-37f51abd55a3c977.png
caption: A polyamide: monomers join through amide links.
```

```figure
src: chemistry/_sme/from-models-to-materials/img/fig-5631b80af4d04c91.png
caption: A polyester needs two monomers with two reactive groups each: a diol and a dicarboxylic acid.
```

- **Polyesters:** a diol + a dicarboxylic acid → ester links (–COO–) + water. Example: PET.
- **Polyamides:** a diamine + a dicarboxylic acid → amide links (–CONH–) + water. Examples: nylon, Kevlar. Proteins are natural polyamides made from amino acids.

## Summary

- Polymers are chains of repeating monomer units.
- Addition: C=C monomers, no by-product; repeating unit = opened-up monomer.
- Plastics persist; recycle, reduce, use biodegradable alternatives.
- HL: condensation polymers (polyesters, polyamides) release water at each link.
